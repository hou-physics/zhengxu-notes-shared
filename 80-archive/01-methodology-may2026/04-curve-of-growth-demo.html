Curve of growth — two aperture methods on a real source

Companion to the method-evolution page and the retrospective on the dropped footprint + IoU diagnostics. Once a mask has been chosen for a detection, the next question is how much flux is inside it, and how the answer changes as the mask grows. The classical tool for this is the curve of growth (CoG): cumulative flux integrated over a monotonically growing aperture, plotted against some "size" parameter. A real source produces a CoG that rises and plateaus; a noise pickup produces a CoG that keeps drifting.

Two ways to grow an aperture

The pipeline has tried two different "growth" definitions, and they disagree on what the right flux estimator is. The demo below puts both side-by-side on the same source.

Method A — σ-dilation. Start at a very high $\sigma$ threshold on the smoothed strong-tracer mom-$0$ map (a tiny mask, only the brightest peak). Lower $\sigma$ in steps. At each step keep only the largest connected component, and integrate the HRL flux over that mask. The "size" parameter is the threshold $\sigma$ itself; the mask shape is data-driven and follows the real CO morphology.

Method B — Ellipse aperture. Fit an ellipse to the bright seed once, then scale it monotonically. The "size" parameter is the semi-major axis $a$ in arcsec; the mask shape is fixed in geometry, independent of where inside the aperture the emission actually lives.

The ellipse is generated entirely by numpy / scipy:

  1. Seed pixels = pixels where the $1''$-smoothed CO mom-$0$ exceeds $6\,\sigma_\text{CO}$. This selects the brightest core of the strong-tracer footprint.
  2. Centroid = intensity-weighted mean of the seed pixel $(x, y)$ positions, with the smoothed-CO value as weight.
  3. Major / minor axes + position angle = eigenvectors of the intensity-weighted covariance matrix of the seed positions (numpy.cov(..., aweights=...) → numpy.linalg.eigh(...)). The eigenvalues give $a_0^2$ and $b_0^2$ (the moment-of-inertia widths); the eigenvector for the larger eigenvalue is the major axis, whose angle to the $+x$ axis is the position angle $\theta$.
  4. Scaling = the slider just multiplies $a_0$ and $b_0$ by a common factor; the shape (axis ratio $b/a$, position angle $\theta$, centre) never changes during the sweep.

This is exactly the "intensity-weighted PCA" used by the canonical $\mathcal{F}_\text{clean}$ recipe (see method-evolution §8). It is the simplest principled way to get a single uniform aperture from the data without any human hand-tuning, but as the demo below makes visible, the choice of weights and seed threshold matter: a $6\,\sigma$ seed threshold picks up only the brightest core and ignores any extended elongation, so the resulting PA can disagree with what the σ-dilated mask traces out at lower thresholds.

The pipeline currently uses Method A for the dilation-CoG baseline- residual diagnostic (baseline_diag.py, 2026-05-17), and an ellipse- plus-spatial-null variant of Method B for the canonical truth flux $\mathcal{F}_\text{clean}$ on confirmed detections. The demo below uses the naive (no spatial-null subtraction) version of Method B so the noise pickup behaviour at large aperture is visible.

The demo

The widget below is a self-contained page. Each panel shows the H$30\alpha$ moment-$0$ of NGC 4945 (the brightest control detection in the sample, $\mathrm{S/N} = 23.5$) with a diverging colormap centred on zero — red = positive integrated line, blue = negative noise, white = zero baseline. Below the image, a slider sweeps the size parameter, and a curve-of-growth mini-plot below each panel shows $F$ versus the size parameter, with the current value highlighted.

The two sliders are independent. Drag both and compare:

  • where does each method's CoG peak, and at what flux value?
  • where does the aperture S/N peak relative to the flux peak? (Hint: it peaks at a smaller aperture — at some point each extra beam of aperture adds more noise than signal.)
  • how does each method respond to noise at large aperture?
  • which method gives a flux that agrees with Toma (2024)? The reference measurement on this source is $9.27$ Jy·km/s (deep data). Integrated over the full line window ($\pm 16$ channels $\approx 333$ km/s, the same window the pipeline uses for its reported flux), both methods here peak at $\sim 8.7$ Jy·km/s — agreeing with Toma to within ALMA's $\sim 10\%$ absolute flux calibration.

The S/N values shown here are computed against an off-source noise $\sigma$ measured directly from the cropped mom-$0$ map (post-baseline subtraction), so they are systematically higher than the catalogue S/N which uses the more conservative pipeline noise definition (per-channel $\sigma$ over line-free channels with ENBW correction). The shape of the curves — peak location, plateau, decline — is the part to read off; the absolute amplitude is illustrative.

What to read off the two curves

Method A — σ-dilation. As $\sigma$ drops from $32$ down to about $5$, the mask grows from the brightest single beam outward along the CO morphology and the CoG rises smoothly — each newly-admitted pixel sits in a region where CO emission is statistically present, so the integrand has a positive mean. The CoG peaks near $\sigma \approx 6$ at $F \approx 8.7$ Jy·km/s (in agreement with Toma's $9.27$); lowering $\sigma$ further pulls the flux back down, because the smoothed-CO statistic between $2\sigma$ and $3\sigma$ admits the noise basin around the disk where the HRL pixels are net-negative. This is the empirical reason the scan's $\sigma$-threshold grid stops at $3\sigma$ on its lower end — going below admits the noise basin and starts eating cumulative flux. The nominal "detection flux" the pipeline reports is the peak/plateau value, not the integral at $\sigma = 2$.

Method B — Ellipse aperture. Watch what happens when the slider goes past $a \approx 6 \text{ arcsec}$: the curve stops rising and starts to fall. This is the canonical failure mode of a naive growing- aperture CoG: as the aperture extends beyond the source, it begins including off-source pixels whose mean is zero on average but is finite for any single realisation; for NGC 4945 those pixels happen to be slightly net-negative, so the cumulative flux drops back down. The peak of the curve is roughly where the aperture matches the source extent, and this is the flux estimator one would quote from this method — but its absolute value is corrupted by the local background, and the appropriate fix is to subtract a same-aperture flux measured at $K$ random spatial-null positions (the canonical $\mathcal{F}_\text{clean}$ recipe shipped 2026-05-17).

Why we have both

Neither method is "right" alone:

  • Method A's plateau is robust only when the source is one connected bright region. On a galaxy where the bright CO is split into several disconnected pieces (a face-on barred spiral, an interacting pair), Method A's connected-component filter picks only the brightest piece and silently drops the rest.
  • Method B's fitted ellipse is reproducible across the sample and yields a defensible systematic uncertainty when paired with a background-subtraction step (next paragraph). On its own — as the demo above shows — it is biased by the local sky once the aperture grows past the source.

The canonical recipe pairs Method B with random-position spatial null subtraction: in addition to the source CoG, the pipeline computes the same growth curve at $K = 16$ random spatial positions across the cube (avoiding the source itself) and averages them into a "background CoG". The clean flux estimator is

$$ F_\text{clean}(a) \;=\; F_\text{source}(a) - \big\langle F_\text{null}(a)\big\rangle_{K=16}, $$

with the per-scale spread $\sigma_{F_\text{clean}}(a) = \text{std}\,F_\text{null}(a)$ giving a non-parametric uncertainty on the corrected CoG. The peak of $F_\text{clean}(a)$ is the number reported in the detection catalogue; the demo above shows only $F_\text{source}(a)$ (no subtraction), which is why it dips after the maximum.

Method A serves a complementary role as the dilation-CoG baseline-residual diagnostic: integrating cumulatively as the mask grows tests whether the per-channel baseline subtraction has left a flat or a sloped residual. A real source produces a CoG that climbs to a plateau; a baseline-residual signature produces a CoG that keeps drifting after the source has been captured.
