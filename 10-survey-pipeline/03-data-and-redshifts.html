Step 2 — Getting the ALMA data

Step 2 — Getting the ALMA data

Everything the survey measures comes out of ALMA archival data. This page describes what we actually download, why exactly those files, and how a fresh download becomes a ready-to-process target.

2a. Querying the archive per target

Tool: the /alma-query package (.claude/skills/alma-query/, Python, built on astroquery.alma) — the project's own archive-search machinery, developed through the detection era and still the front door for every target.

For a galaxy it:

  1. resolves the coordinates and redshift;
  2. cone-searches the ALMA Science Archive for all public observations;
  3. parses each observation's frequency windows (frequency_support) and keeps those covering a redshifted recombination line plus a bright tracer line;
  4. applies the archive-metadata filters (single pointing only: is_mosaic = False);
  5. ranks the surviving observations (beam size, sensitivity, same-window line pairs) and writes a per-target download script (step-1_download.py / download_urls.txt) — large downloads are run by hand, by policy, so disk budget stays under human control.

2b. What is downloaded — and what is deliberately not

An ALMA archive entry contains many products. We download exactly two kinds of files per spectral window:

file what it is why we need it
*.cube.I.pbcor.fits the calibrated spectral image cube, primary-beam corrected — true sky fluxes at every position and velocity all flux measurements
*.pb.fits.gz the primary-beam response map — the telescope's sensitivity pattern across the field of view to undo/redo the correction (below) and to know where the data ends

Not downloaded: raw visibilities and calibration tables (tens of times larger; only needed if we re-image ourselves — see Step 3), and continuum/auxiliary images. This "selective diet" is 3–50× smaller than the full tarballs (e.g. NGC 2369: 1024 GB → 21 GB) and is what made a 16-target batch (~345 GB) feasible. Script: target_list_build/wave1_selective_sizes.py computes the per-target sizes; wave1_downloads.py writes the download kits.

Why the primary-beam map matters: the pbcor cube has correct fluxes but spatially varying noise (the correction amplifies the map edges); multiplying it by the primary-beam map recovers a flat-noise view of the same data. The pipeline uses each form where it is the right tool — flat-noise for thresholding (aperture construction), pbcor for flux integration.

2c. Organizing a target: canonical names

Raw product filenames are long member/spw strings. Onboarding (script: target_list_build/wave1_onboard.py) reads every cube's frequency range from its header, identifies which lines fall in band at the galaxy's redshift, and applies a fixed pairing rule (no judgment calls):

  1. prefer a dataset containing both the recombination line and a tracer (identical calibration and pointing);
  2. among candidates, the sharpest recombination-line beam within 0.3″–8″;
  3. tracer preference: CO(2-1) > CO(1-0) > CS(2-1) > HCO⁺(1-0) > HCN(1-0) > ¹³CO(2-1) — brightest first;
  4. calibrator-field images that ship in some tarballs (quasar pointings, _ph/_bp in the filename) are excluded by rule — an early run paired two galaxies onto quasar fields before this filter existed.

The chosen cubes get canonical symlinks — work_dir/{GALAXY}/{GALAXY}_{line}_pbcor.fits etc. — so every downstream script addresses any galaxy the same way. A per-target manifest records what was chosen and why.

2d. Redshift verification (the trace-back rule)

The catalog redshift has one job: place the lines inside the receiver band. The measurement redshift is re-derived from our own data — the tracer line's centre in the cube — accurate to a few km/s.

Why this is a rule and not paranoia — a real case. IRAS 19542+1110 first returned "no tracer detected" although literature CO detections exist. The full-band spectrum showed CO(2-1) at overwhelming significance, 530 km/s away from the catalog prediction: the catalog value (a 1995 optical measurement, copied forward by later papers) was simply wrong. Re-deriving the redshift from our own line rescued the target. Hence:

Every "no tracer detected" outcome triggers a trace-back: plot the full-band spectrum. Line elsewhere → wrong catalog redshift → re-derive from our own data and re-run. Genuinely no line → human decision (NGC 55, a dwarf whose CS line is below the data's sensitivity, was excluded this way).

Redshift provenance (catalog vs. measured-from-own-CO) is recorded per target in the download manifest.
